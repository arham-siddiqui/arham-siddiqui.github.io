Santa Clara Coding Association

Mar 2021 - Jun 2025

Skills

Overview

I founded Santa Clara Coding Association as a city-wide computer science education initiative to make coding more accessible to students through clubs, workshops, hackathons, and structured curriculum. Through SCCA, I reached 1,500+ students and focused on helping beginners move from passive exposure to active programming through hands-on lessons in Python, Java, and core computational thinking.

Problem

I saw that many students were interested in computer science but did not have a clear entry point, especially if their school did not offer deep programming pathways or if they were intimidated by competitive coding environments. My goal with SCCA was to create an approachable local pathway where students could learn syntax, debugging, algorithms, and project building in a peer-led environment before moving into more advanced CS opportunities.

Methods

I combined recurring CS clubs with event-based learning. I organized curriculum around progressive skill development: introductory programming concepts, control flow, functions, data structures, object-oriented programming, and applied projects. My Python lessons emphasized rapid iteration and beginner-friendly problem solving, while my Java sessions supported students preparing for more formal CS coursework and AP-style programming patterns.

I used hackathons and workshops as applied checkpoints. Students practiced translating ideas into working prototypes, collaborating in teams, debugging under time constraints, and presenting technical work. Operationally, running the association required lesson planning, event logistics, mentor coordination, student outreach, and classroom facilitation across multiple cohorts.

What Was Built

I built SCCA into a repeatable student-led education model: curriculum modules, classroom workshops, coding club structures, hackathon programming, and mentorship systems. I designed the program to meet students at different skill levels, from first-time programmers learning variables and loops to more advanced students experimenting with Java, project design, and machine-learning concepts.

Results

I reached 1,500+ students through city-wide CS clubs, hackathons, and coding curriculum. Beyond raw participation, the important result for me was building a local technical community where students could practice programming in a low-friction environment, ask questions, work through bugs, and see computer science as something they could actually build with.

Conclusion

Santa Clara Coding Association became one of my leadership projects centered on technical education systems. I combined curriculum design, teaching, event operations, mentorship, and community building into a sustained CS access program. My experience with SCCA shaped how I think about technical communication: good engineering education is not just about knowing the material, but about sequencing it clearly, creating useful practice loops, and building confidence through real projects.
