Abstract—This research explores the reactionary effect of
rodents to medical testing. The purpose of this project is to
assist scientists and pharmaceuticals with techniques that can
improve the reliability of drug discovery and aid companies
in the development process of drugs and vaccines by proving
the efficiency of supervised machine-learning models in such a
setting. 13 features representing rodent reactions to drugs were
artificially created and varied to simulate the results of the
specimen observation period conducted by scientists in a range
of 72 hours post-injection. We created two models, an SVC ()
and KNN (), our KNN having a 40% higher accuracy score
on average, ultimately having 100% accuracy. After training
multiple models with different hyperparameters, our algorithm
is capable of pairing a new set of post-injection observations
with a previously tested drug with 100% accuracy. We noticed
significant differences in accuracy between the SVC and KNN
models, with our KNNs having a 40% higher accuracy score on
average. Additionally, we increased the size of our dataset from
13x1800 to 13x6000 to evaluate the models on a larger dataset.
When using the Manhattan Distance Formula and the Euclidean
Distance Formula, we noticed that Manhattan was the optimal
formula for our algorithm. By identifying an effective drug-
matching model coupled with supplemental research exploring
its advantages, drawbacks, and optimal conditions, scientists and
companies can use it as a reference point to further direct their
development efforts. Such assistance to the development process
will consequently reduce the time required, thus eliminating
increased costs and delays in treatment for patients.
I. I NTRODUCTION
The development of medical drugs is a complex and time-
consuming process that typically requires 5-10 years, if not
more, for most professional cases. This process is a multi-
faceted undertaking requiring multiple stages, such as the
conduction of lengthy laboratory testing to assess the safety,
effectiveness, and efficiency of the product. Additionally,
scientists must conduct extensive testing on animals before
finally, being approved for human testing where scientists
must conduct detailed experiments for 3 different phases. This
tedious process consequently poses significant challenges for
the medical industry as well as broad implications for society.
II. B ACKGROUND RESEARCH
Such problems include the increased cost associated with
the development process, delay in treatment, competitive prod-
ucts, and risk of failure. The median research and development
investment for a new drug to be brought to market was
estimated to be $985.3 million [4], a considerable sum that
is further increased depending upon the length of the devel-
opment process. This cost is translated over-the-counter to the
consumer, which can unfortunately make certain medications
unaffordable for some. This exorbitant price tag leads to
financial strain, overall distrust in the medical system, and
hinders the use of necessary medications resulting in serious
consequences for individuals’ health and well being. On the
topic of the health and well being of the patient, the length
of a medications’ development process can yield obvious
consequences to those waiting for treatment, as they are left
to suffer the symptoms of illness.
More specifically, for those with serious or life-threatening
conditions, the prolonged roll-out of such medications is
simply beyond frustrating [9]. A time-intensive production
process also induces the competitive nature of the medical
industry. With 9 of the top 10 pharmaceutical companies
based in the US and most being active in drug production,
research, and development, it goes without saying that the US
medical industry brews aggressive competition [10]. Such an
environment urges companies to use a more shortened, yet
efficient, development process to break from competitors and
earn a spot on the shelf.
Drug production, no matter the scale, will likely result in
failure in some form [5]. Such failure prompts reiteration and
improvement in the overall product. Failure during a drawn-out
production process can cause major setbacks for the company
and patients waiting for the treatment, as the time and money
spent can never be replaced. Therefore, it is ideal that such
flaws are brought to light early in the process, or as soon as
possible. With a more efficient development process, failure
points can be exposed quicker, and improvements can be
applied to proceed with the process in a timely manner.
This evidence points to a quicker development process being
desirable for medical companies seeking the optimal cost,
efficiency, and competitive nature of their drugs. Machine
learning, the sub-field of artificial intelligence involving the
training of algorithms to make decisions based on patterns
in large amounts of data, can be applied to enhance the
drug development process in multiple contexts. This research
particularly explores the use of machine learning to help
identify matching drugs based on the reactionary effects of
medical testing on rodents. More specifically, we conduct
a comparative analysis between various classifiers, model
architectures, as well as data-set manipulation to maximize
the efficiency of a supervised multi-class classification model
in this setting.

V. C RITERIA AND CONSTRAINTS
Our model must be able to accurately match the reactionary
effects of a given drug to that of the closest preexisting
approved drug from one of our five drugs, or classes, with an
accuracy of at least 90% (percent of total test data identified
correctly).
This accuracy will be calculated for each class by using
the number of true positives and true negatives divided by
the number of total classifications. The true positives and true
negatives are the number of correct classifications within a
given class, whereas the remaining values (false negatives and
false positives) consist of the incorrect classifications within
that class.
Originally, we planned on adding time constraints to prove
our program’s efficiency, but figured that in a real-world setting
as long as our time to run was under 30 minutes, (which it
was), time wouldn’t be a practical issue to solve.
Metrics such as precision, recall, and f1-score are also
analyzed. In regards to these metrics, precedence will be
placed on precision, as minimizing false positive matches is
of particular importance in such a sophisticated setting where
a mismatched drug could monumentally impair the course of
production.

VIII. D ATASET
The dataset used is synthetically created and composed
of 13 numerical features mentioned previously. These values
are based on true and thoroughly observed baseline accepted
values as explained in the Feature Explanation Document [see
Appendix A].
Each feature is further broken into 3 subcategories: average,
high, and low. These benchmarks are pointers for the different
possible ranges of the respective feature. The high and low
ranges are derived from the axioms defined in the explanation
document, and are consistent with real-life principles. In
perspective, this paper and the research conducted is all meant
to simulate real world possibility. Since this dataset does not
include any industry-specific drugs, the differentiating feature
ranges are applied to model real-world variation observed in
reactionary effects. We synthesized 6 artificial drugs labeled
Drug A - F. Each drug contains all 13 features with different
variations of said ranges. These variations can be seen in
the Drug Description Document [see Appendix B]. These
variations were applied to our dataset by using this equation,
where R is a random decimal value between 0 and 1, u is the
upper limit to our range, and l is the lower limit of our range,
to create said random values:
R ∗ (u − l) + l (1)
The category listed on each drug is simply the correspond-
ing letter in its name. This feature has been implemented to
allow for the training and testing of our data in the methods
section of this paper.
The breakdown for this dataset is as follows. Each drug
contains 13 features, each of which belonging to one of the
3 different ranges, and there are 300 samples of each feature.
Using the “concat()” function from the pandas library, stacking
all drug datasets together creates a matrix sized 1800 x 14.
To process this dataset into a working environment, we start
by reading in each drug individually and then stack all drugs
into a single dataframe. After doing so, all duplicate values
are checked and eliminated to prevent incorrect or misleading
predictions based on overfitting. Duplicate identification was
done through the “duplicated()” pandas function. Elimination
was done through the “drop duplicates()” pandas function.
For this dataset, we used an 80/20 training and testing set
split. This was done with the use of the “train test split()”
scikit-learn function. This leaves a training feature matrix sized
1440 x 13, testing features 360 x 13, training labels 1440 x
1, and testing labels 360 x 1.
Throughout the course of this research, this dataset is
altered to analyze performance results after alterations. Feature
extraction and other forms of dataset manipulation will be
expanded upon in the methods section of this paper.
IX. M ETHODS
SVM
We started by using an SVM, or support vector machine.
This is a simple machine-learning algorithm that is used for
baseline training. The goal of this model, more specifically an
SVC (support vector classifier) shown in Figure 1, is to create
a maximum margin classifier based on the data given, which is
a hyperplane that maximally separates two categories of data,
to provide a classification when given new data.
Fig. 1.
Since the problem of this research pertains to multi-class
classification, using the rudimentary SVC model will not work,
as they are intrinsically meant for binary classification. There
are multiple ways to tackle this problem. More specifically,
OVO (one vs one) and OV A/OVR (one vs all/rest), are two of
the most commonly used approaches. OVO, or the one vs one
approach, creates a binary classifier for each pair of classes.
For example, a 3-class classification problem would require 6
classifiers as shown in Figure 2.
Fig. 2.
The OV A/OVR, or one vs all/rest approach, is executed
exactly as the name suggests. A hyperplane is derived for
each class to separate it from all the others combined. For this
Fig. 3.
approach, the same 3-class classification problem will require
only 3 classifiers, which can be seen in Figure 3.
After an understanding of both of these methods, we de-
cided to run our model using the OVO approach first. To do so,
we implemented the Support Vector Classifier as SVC from the
Scikit-Learn Library. We set the “decision function shape”
parameter to “ovo” to specify our approach. The next step
was to fit the model with our previously split data. This was
done through the “fit()” function from the Scikit-Learn Library.
As we were training the model, our “X train” and “y train”
matrices were passed in as arguments for the “fit” function.
The last step was generating predictions on our testing set. To
do so, we used the “predict()” function from the Scikit-Learn
Library, passing in our allocated testing data: “X test”.
After analyzing the results of the SVC (OVO) we decided
to use the SVC with the OVR approach. To reiterate, the goal
of the OVR model is to derive a hyperplane for each class to
separate it from all the others combined, hence the name: one
vs rest. We utilized the OneVsRestClassifier from the Scikit-
Learn Multiclass Library to implement this model. We fit this
model onto our previously split dataset. Just as we did with
the previous model, we used the “predict()” function from
the Scikit Learn Library to make predictions on our “X test”
matrix. We stored these predictions in “y pred”.
KNN
The next model used is a KNN, or K Nearest Neighbors
model that doesn’t require training. Classification is achieved
by first plotting all data samples onto a plane. When a test
sample is introduced as a data point, the distance between it
and all the other training samples is calculated using distance
metrics, the most common being Euclidean and Manhattan
distance. After this distance is calculated, the model selects K
- the number of “nearest neighbors” - of the closest training
samples to determine the prediction of the classification.
This is done through a simple majority-based voting al-
gorithm. KNN is advantageous in this setting as it can dif-
ferentiate well between classes that have stark differences
between features, such as Drug A and Drug B, the former
composed of all low values, whereas the latter being composed
of all high values. This model, however, starts to falter in the
classification between classes that have overlapping values,
as certain data samples can be misidentified because of their
similar positioning on the plane. We applied this model to
our dataset and altered the number of nearest neighbors along
with the power parameter which controls the type of distance
calculation used. A visual representation of this model can be
viewed in Figure 4.
Fig. 4.
We set the two main parameters, n (amount of neighbors)
and p (type of distance calculation) to the default value, which
was 5 neighbors and the Euclidean Distance Formula. As done
before, we fit this model on our training data from our split
dataset. The model predictions were stored in “y pred”, which
were made from the “predict()” function from the Scikit-Learn
Library.
After obtaining a significant improvement in the accuracy
and precision scores of our model, we decided it would be
best to capitalize on the performance of the KNN model. To
do so efficiently, we decided to visualize all combinations
of reasonable n-neighbor values with either Euclidean or
Manhattan Distance. We created this python program to figure
out the most optimal hyperparameters which ended up being 3
neighbors with the Manhattan Distance Formula. After apply-
ing these parameters to our model, we got a 98.33% accuracy
score. This was extremely impressive, and upon increasing the
size of our dataset, we achieved a 100% accuracy score.
A visualization of the hyperparameter accuracies can be
seen in Figure 5.
Fig. 5.

Fig. 6.
A model with 100% prediction accuracy will have a con-
fusion matrix with a clear diagonal descending from left to
right. This is true as all other values in the matrix will be zero
and only the correct classifications will be colored in. An ideal
confusion matrix can be seen in Figure 7.
Fig. 7.
Comparing the matrix in Figure 6 to our matrix in Figure
7, we can locate the specific classes that our SVC (OVO)
inaccurately predicted. Looking back on an ideal matrix, there
should only be one colored box for each class, whereas our
matrix has 2 colored boxes for class 1 and 3 colored boxes

outliers but major clusters of classifications ranging from 57-
66 incorrect classifications per misplaced prediction. Using the
confusion matrix, we can ultimately determine that our SVC
(OVO) commonly confused class 1 with class 3, and class 5
with classes 0 and 2. Converting these classes into our data set
means that each drug is equivalent to its corresponding class
index. This means that Drug A is Class 0, Drug B is Class
1, etc. Translating the results from our confusion matrix, we
can infer that our SVC (OVO) commonly confused Drug B
with Drug D, and Drug F with Drug A and Drug C. To gain
insight into the model’s difficulties in distinguishing between
these classes, we examined the Drug Description Document

have caused issues in our classification because of very similar

we observed that our model read all three drugs as Drug
F, most likely due to very similar features and overlapping
ranges. We inferred that this is a viable cause for incorrect
predictions as this could very well happen in a real-world
testing environment.

Using the “classification report()” from the Scikit-Learn
Metrics Library and passing “y pred” and “y test” (the

Analyzing these metrics, we can see that OVR has a very
high precision accuracy for drugs A and C. Additionally, it has
Fig. 10.
consistently good values of precision for the remaining drugs,
scoring an average of 0.63. Within our testing, precision is very
important as this value refers to the proportion of true positive
predictions (the number of correct positive predictions) out
of all positive predictions made by a model (true positive
predictions plus false positive predictions). A high precision
value indicates that a model is good at identifying true positive
instances and has a low number of false positive predictions.
While OVR had an average precision rating of 0.63 or 63%,
OVO had a rating of only 0.49 or 49%. OVO had perfect
precision for only one drug, drug e, while it struggled greatly
with drugs a, c, and d. This could be due to OVO only
performing one-to-one comparisons, limiting the possibility
to see other values and making connections and predictions
between them. Conversely, OVR makes comparisons from one
value to all of the other values, allowing it to make proper
connections and predictions based on the entire data set at
once. This limitation caused OVO to score an overall precision
accuracy of .49 which is much too low for our needs.
In conclusion, we determined that for the SVC model, OVR
is the preferred option. Not only was OVR’s precision higher,
but it also had better recall and f1 scores, making it the optimal
choice for our testing.

Blood Pressure (mmHg):
What: Blood pressure is a measure of the force of blood
against the walls of your arteries as it is pumped around
your body by your heart. It is expressed as two numbers:
systolic blood pressure (the higher number) and diastolic blood
pressure (the lower number). The units of blood pressure are
millimeters of mercury (mmHg).
Why: High blood pressure levels are major risks for both
heart disease and strokes. Keeping track of this is greatly
important due to the severe health risks that can ensue with
consistently high blood pressure.
Levels: Low: 70-90 mmHg Good: 90-115 mmHg High: 115-
130 mmHg
Heart Beats (BPM):
What: Heart rate is the measure of heart contractions within
a period, this can help provide insight into the overall health
of the animal.
Why: Heart rate offers insight into the overall health of
an animal and is commonly used to quickly determine if
something is wrong. Maintaining proper heart functions is
crucial for any animal’s survival, if any vaccine or drug
changed these functions for the worse the animal’s life may be
at risk. By carefully monitoring heartbeats and abnormalities
in the cardiovascular systems we can better determine the rats’
overall health.
Levels: Low: 450-549 beats/min Good: 550-650 beats/min
High: 651-750 beats/min
Weight Gain/Loss over 72 hours (% of body weight):
What: Sudden weight gain or loss could be caused by an
under or overactive thyroid gland, which releases hormones
that control metabolism. The rats could also be affected in
other ways causing them to lose all appetite.
Why: Quickly gaining or losing weight in a short amount
of time can be a symptom of a variety of problems within the
body.
Levels:
Low: - 2-3% of total body weight Good: ± 0-1% of total
body weight High: +2-3% of total body weight
Body Temperature (C):
What: Fever is one of the body’s first reactions to infection
because when the immune system notices a virus in your body,
it alerts the hypothalamus to increase the body’s temperature.
Why: Keeping track of internal temperatures can give in-
sight into the rat’s reaction to each drug. Changes in internal
temperatures will allow us to determine what similar reactions
may occur in humans after being injected with the trial drugs.
Levels: Low: 36-36.8 °C Good: 37.5-37.8 °C High: 38-39.8
°C
Cholesterol Levels (mg/dL):
What: Cholesterol is a waxy substance made by the liver
and found in all cells of your body. Animal cells don’t have
cell walls like plant cells, so they use this substance to create
a structurally integral foundation within the body.
Why: Having high levels of cholesterol can cause it to build
up in your arteries, creating plaque that can stuff up and block
blood flow. Low levels of cholesterol can increase the risk of
having a hemorrhagic stroke, as well as other blood-related
problems within the body.
Levels: Low: 45.18 ± 2.64 mg/dl Good: 49.64 ± 1.82 mg/dl
High: 52 ± 1.54 mg/dl
Glomerular Filtration Rate (ml/min/100 g body weight)
What: Glomerular filtration rate (GFR) is a test used to
determine how well the kidneys are functioning. Specifically,
it estimates how much blood passes through the glomeruli each
minute, glomeruli are tiny filters within the kidneys that filter
waste from the blood.
Why: If our rats suffer from drastic changes in their kidney
functions, kidney failure may be imminent and the rats may
even die due to these failures.
Levels: Low: 0.5 ml/min/100g body weight Good: 1.00
ml/min/100g body weight High: 1.5 ml/min/100g body weight
Glucose Levels (mg/dL):
What: Glucose is a simple sugar that is necessary within our
blood, as it provides us energy once broken down. Glucose can
be attained through consuming foods.
Why: Your blood not only provides oxygen to the body but
also glucose, with low or high levels of glucose permanent
damage could ensue upon a multitude of important bodily
functions such as the eyes, kidneys, and nerves. Monitoring
this is important as any drastic and continual changes after
receiving a drug would not be safe for use in humans.
Levels: Low: 30-50 mg/dL Good: 50-135 mg/dL High: 135-
150 mg/dL
WBC (White Blood Cell Count):
What: WBC helps combat infections and viruses that
threaten the rats’ health. If a vaccine affected this balance
and caused a change to this count, the rats’ health would be
at risk, therefore keeping track of WBC is an essential part of
our testing.
Why: As our simulated rats will all have a similar mass,
the number of white blood cells within their body will help
determine the potency of the immune system, and how it may
react against foreign substances that attack the body.
Levels: Low: ¡ 18,000 Good: 18,000 - 22,000 High: ¿ 22,000
Within the large category of WBC, there are many different
subcategories or types of WBC which all play an important
role in maintaining the health of the rats in our testing. To best
track the health of the rats, keeping tabs on each category of
WBC is essential. If one category strays low or high in value
the rats’ health could be at risk.
Neutrophils (% of WBC):
What:
Within the blood Neutrophils are the first responders, they
destroy dangerous and infectious microorganisms by ingesting
them and releasing enzymes that then kill them. The neu-
trophils also help boost the response of other white blood cells
keeping infections and overall health risks at a minimum.
Why:
Neutrophils make up a majority of the total white blood
cells in the body, so keeping healthy levels of neutrophils is,
arguably, the most important as fewer other white blood cells
are helping to make up for the losses. High levels of neu-
trophils can cause fevers and recurring infections, while low
levels can cause extreme exhaustion and continual unending
infections.
Levels: Low: 30-40% WBC Good: 40-60% of WBC High:
60-70% WBC Lymphocytes (% of WBC):
What:
Lymphocytes are produced within the bone marrow and
have many important functions within the body such as
producing antibodies and helping kill tumors and cancers.
Additionally, lymphocytes help the immune system remember
every antigen it comes in contact with, allowing the body to
more efficiently fight it off during the next encounter.
Why:
Without proper levels of Lymphocytes, the body may be
subject to the wrath of dangerous cancers. To help prevent
this we keep track of the % composition of Lymphocytes in
the blood after new vaccines have been injected.
Levels: Low: 15-20% WBC Good: 20-40% WBC High: 40-
45% WBC
Monocytes (% of WBC):
What:
Like lymphocytes, monocytes are made within the bone
marrow and mainly help find, and destroy germs and viruses
that enter the bloodstream. Once they identify a dangerous
invader they begin to eliminate it but also call for help from
other white blood cells.
Why:
Ensuring the body can quickly identify viruses that pose
a threat to the health of the animal is crucial. Monitoring
monocytes, and ensuring they are kept at the appropriate level
for healthy activity is highly important.
Levels: Low: 0.1-2% WBC Good: 2-8% WBC High: 8-10%
WBC
Eosinophils (% of WBC):
What:
Eosinophils, like many of the other white blood cell types,
are formed solely in the bone marrow. After fully maturing,
the cells begin to fulfill their intended function of fighting off
both infections and regulating the inflammatory process in the
body. One of the special functions of eosinophils is responding
to allergic reactions and disorders.
Why:
Eosinophiles’ important role of moderating inflamed areas
and fighting allergic disorders is essential in case of health
risks. Without proper levels of eosinophils, the body is open
and vulnerable to a multitude of life-threatening illnesses
or diseases. High levels of eosinophils are telltale signs of
parasitic infection or cancer. These major health risks are
many reasons we keep track of eosinophils within the immune
system.
Levels: Low: 0.1-1% WBC Good: 1-4% WBC High: 4-6%
WBC
Basophils (% of WBC):
What:
Basophils are the least prominent type of white blood cells
in the body, making up less than 1% of the total count,
on top of this they have a very short lifespan of only 1-2
days. Regardless, basophils still play an important role in the
immune system as they are the only type of white blood cells
that can produce and release the enzyme histamine which helps
fight against allergic reactions and asthma attacks.
Why:
Because basophils are the only cell type that can release his-
tamine that inherently makes them important for the immune
system. Having high levels of basophils is very dangerous and
can be a sign of leukemia and autoimmune disease. Measuring
levels will allow us to see any dangerous fluctuations after
introducing vaccines.
Levels: Low: 0.1-0.5% WBC Good: 0.5-1% WBC High:
1-2% WBC
