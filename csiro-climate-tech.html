Overview

My CSIRO experience centered on research for a USA-Australia Climate Technology MOU, with emphasis on how machine learning and data systems can support climate infrastructure decisions. I connected technical climate-tech domains such as grid optimization, emissions quantification, ESG risk assessment, and AI policy into a policy-facing research product. My goal was to understand where AI could create measurable operational value while still fitting within public-sector, research, and international collaboration constraints.

Problem

I had to bridge multiple layers of climate technology strategy: energy-system operations, carbon-accounting methodology, financial and ESG risk, policy incentives, and cross-border implementation. I treated a purely technical model as insufficient if policymakers could not interpret it or integrate it into institutional workflows. My work therefore focused on translating ML-enabled capabilities into a practical MOU context, where recommendations needed to be technically grounded, legible to non-engineering stakeholders, and connected to realistic deployment pathways.

Research Areas

In the grid-optimization component, I focused on how AI systems can improve energy-system efficiency through forecasting, dispatch support, demand-response modeling, and operational decision support. In a climate-tech context, I framed this as using data-driven models to understand load variability, renewable generation intermittency, storage constraints, and grid stability tradeoffs. My research framing emphasized that ML should complement physical grid models rather than replace them, especially when decisions affect reliability and public infrastructure.

In the emissions-quantification component, I examined how data pipelines and statistical models can turn fragmented activity data into more reliable carbon estimates. I thought through measurement, reporting, and verification workflows: where emissions factors come from, how uncertainty propagates, how organizations validate claims, and how AI can help detect inconsistencies or automate parts of carbon-accounting review. I also connected those technical measurements to ESG risk, including climate exposure, reporting quality, regulatory risk, and stakeholder trust.

Methods

I contributed research synthesis and technical framing across ML-enabled grid optimization, emissions modeling, and ESG-risk use cases. My work involved identifying where machine learning was technically appropriate, separating near-term deployable tools from speculative capabilities, and mapping each capability to institutional constraints such as data availability, interpretability, validation, policy alignment, and cross-agency collaboration. The MOU context required me to compress complex technical ideas into clear language that could support strategic decision-making.

What Was Built

My output was not a standalone software product; it was a research and strategy contribution for climate-technology collaboration. I helped frame how AI could support grid operations, emissions transparency, and ESG-risk assessment inside a bilateral technology agenda. Technically, my value was in designing the connective tissue between model capabilities and implementation reality: what data would be needed, what outputs stakeholders could trust, and what forms of validation would be necessary before deployment.

Conclusion

This experience strengthened my ability to reason about AI systems beyond model performance alone. In climate technology, I learned that a useful ML system has to survive contact with infrastructure constraints, policy incentives, messy data, and governance requirements. My CSIRO MOU work sat exactly at that intersection, combining technical research with leadership, communication, and policy-aware systems thinking.
